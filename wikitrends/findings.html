What we found

The main test came back null. Before collecting any data, we wrote down one test and the rule for calling it. Did words like “genocide”, “apartheid”, “occupation” and “colonial” grow more, on average, in 422 articles about Israel, Jews and Zionism than in articles about other countries and conflicts? By that rule the answer is not supported. The average article did not move more than its comparison.

And yet a small set of heavily read pages changed. We then collected every article in the topic: 10,320 articles in all. 4,642 are on the topic and 5,678 are for comparison. Only 3.9% of topic articles gained critical vocabulary in their opening paragraph. Those pages carry 15% of topic reading. In comparison articles the two figures are 1.5% and 5%. An average over articles barely moves. What readers meet did.

The longer version.

  1. Between 2021 and 2026, English Wikipedia’s Israel and Palestine articles took on more of the vocabulary of legal accusation and of 1948: genocide, ethnic cleansing, expulsion, occupation. Judaism articles did not move. Comparison articles on other countries and conflicts moved much less.
  2. One rise, then a fall. New critical terms per 1,000 topic edits went from about 7 before the war to about 16 through 2024. By 2026 the rate was back to 5 or 6.
  3. The change is concentrated. The 3.9% of topic articles whose opening paragraph changed carry 15% of topic reading.
  4. The change came as added text, and 89% of it is not about recent events. Established editors wrote it. The same pages also gained protection, formal discussions (RfCs), framing categories and links to core concepts such as the Nakba. A few high-traffic opening paragraphs now state contested labels in Wikipedia’s own voice: Gaza genocide, Israel, Israeli apartheid, Nakba, Zionism.
  5. Jewish-history vocabulary was kept. Sources cited in opening paragraphs shifted only slightly. Other language editions mostly did not follow. We found no sign of synchronized tag-teaming, and no sign that blocked accounts did more than their share.

What this cannot tell you. Counting words measures emphasis, not truth. The word “genocide” can appear more often simply because a court case exists. The data cannot tell us whether the new wording is accurate. It cannot tell us what editors intended, or whether anyone coordinated off Wikipedia. “Not supported” means “not shown”, not “nothing happened”.
