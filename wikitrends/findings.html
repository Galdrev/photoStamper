What we found

The main test came back null. Before collecting any data, we wrote down one test and the rule for calling it. Did words like “genocide”, “apartheid”, “occupation” and “colonial” grow more, on average, in 422 articles about Israel, Jews and Zionism than in articles about other countries and conflicts? By that rule the answer is not supported. The average article did not move more than its comparison.

And yet a small set of heavily read pages changed. We then collected every article in the topic: 10,320 articles, 4,642 on the topic and 5,678 for comparison. Only 3.9% of topic articles gained critical vocabulary in their opening paragraph. Those pages carry 15% of topic reading, against 1.5% and 5% in comparison articles. An average over articles barely moves. What readers meet did.

The longer version.

  1. Between 2021 and 2026, English Wikipedia’s Israel and Palestine articles took on more of the vocabulary of legal accusation and of 1948 (genocide, ethnic cleansing, expulsion, occupation). Judaism articles did not move, and comparison articles on other countries and conflicts moved much less.
  2. One wave: new critical terms per 1,000 topic edits went from about 7 before the war to about 16 through 2024, and back to 5 or 6 in 2026.
  3. It is concentrated: the 3.9% of topic articles whose opening paragraph changed carry 15% of topic reading.
  4. It came as added text, 89% of it not about recent events, written by established editors on pages that also gained protection, formal discussions (RfCs), framing categories and links to core concepts such as the Nakba. A few high-traffic opening paragraphs (Gaza genocide, Israel, Israeli apartheid, Nakba, Zionism) now state contested labels in Wikipedia’s own voice.
  5. Jewish-history vocabulary was kept, sources in opening paragraphs shifted only slightly, other language editions mostly did not follow, and we found no sign of synchronized tag-teaming or of blocked accounts doing more than their share.

What this cannot tell you. Counting words measures emphasis, not truth: “genocide” can appear more because a court case exists. The data cannot tell us whether the new wording is accurate, what editors intended, or whether anyone coordinated off-wiki. “Not supported” means “not shown”, not “nothing happened”.
