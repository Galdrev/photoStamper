# How the change happens and who drives it

## 1. At the same level of editing, Israel/Palestine articles change twice as often as other contested topics  [STRONG]

**What we see:** Mass tier (9,175 articles with all three snapshots). Bin articles by how many edits they got. In every bin, topic articles gained critical terms after the war 2-4x as often as comparison articles: 8% vs 2% in the 4th decile, 30% vs 11% in the 9th, 41% vs 19% in the busiest tenth. Among the busiest fifth of each project, Palestine (57%) and Israel (44%) lead. The next are other live conflicts: Ukraine 30%, Armenia 30%, Bosnia 27%, Taiwan 24%, Turkey 23%. Judaism sits at 15%, with Greece, India and Hinduism at 5-8%.

**Chart:** grouped line or dot plot; x: edit-activity decile (1 = least edited), y: share of articles gaining critical terms, Jul 2023 to Nov 2026, series: topic vs comparison, data: chart_data/activity_decile_gain.json, chart_data/busiest_by_project.json

**So what:** Being edited more is not the explanation. Editing turns into this kind of change more readily on Israel/Palestine pages than on pages about other conflicts. For journalists, 'more editors showed up' is not enough of an explanation. The question is what those editors did differently.

**Caveat:** Edit counts cover the whole 2021-2026 window, not post-war only, and the deciles pool both groups.

## 2. Readers came for a month; editors stayed for a year and a half  [SUGGESTIVE]

**What we see:** For 22 existing topic key articles, Oct 2023 pageviews were 22x their pre-war level. They were back to ~2-3x within three months and ~1x by 2026. Edits rose less (4.7x) but stayed at ~1.7-2.5x for 15 months, through Jan 2025. Edits per 100k views fell from 12.8 before the war to about the same in the first six months, as readers outran editors. They then rose to ~30 afterwards. In Zionism, Israeli–Palestinian conflict and Hamas, edits per view ran 3-4x the pre-war rate. Comparison key articles stayed flat; their views fell slowly, in line with the Wikipedia-wide decline. Month-to-month changes in views and edits correlate only in the same month (r=0.24 topic, 0.18 comparison), with no lead or lag.

**Chart:** two-line index chart, one panel per group; x: month 2023-01 to 2026-09, y: index (pre-war mean = 1), pageviews and edits, data: chart_data/views_vs_edits_index.json

**So what:** Reader attention lit the fire, but the rewriting happened long after the readers had left. That is also when fewer people were watching. The lasting changes to leads were written in 2024-25, not in the news peak.

**Caveat:** Only 37 deep-panel articles have monthly pageviews, and edits are counted from weekly bins.

**Examples:**
- *Zionism*: edits per 100k views: 10.9 before the war, 4.0 in the first six months, 42.8 after

## 3. On topic articles an edit surge turns into change; on comparison articles it does not  [SUGGESTIVE]

**What we see:** Deep panel, 332 existing topic articles and 90 comparison articles, split into quartiles by post-war edit surge. In the top topic quartile (median 2.6x more edits per quarter), 52% gained critical vocabulary. In the bottom quartile it was 31%. Comparison articles show no gradient: 39%, 55%, 41%, 35%.

**Chart:** paired bar chart; x: surge quartile, y: share gaining critical terms (full text), series: topic vs comparison, data: chart_data/surge_quartile_gain.json

**So what:** This independently confirms the known 'driven by edit surges' finding. A surge in the comparison set is ordinary churn. A surge in the topic set carries a direction.

**Caveat:** Only 90 comparison articles, and they are much busier at baseline (median 718 vs 105 edits).

## 4. On topic pages, change comes with talk-page discussion, not with edit wars  [SUGGESTIVE]

**What we see:** Deep panel, existing articles with 20 or more edits, split into thirds by talk-page edits per article edit. Among topic articles (n=296), the share gaining critical vocabulary rises from 22% in the low-talk third to 41% and then 60% in the high-talk third. The rise holds within both halves by edit volume: 22%, 28%, 42% in the less-edited half and 23%, 53%, 65% in the more-edited half. Comparison articles (n=90) show no such rise: 47%, 43%, 37%. Together with card 7 (low-revert pages change most), this shows the change was discussed rather than fought over.

**Chart:** grouped bar; x: talk-per-edit tercile, y: share gaining critical terms, series: topic (split by edit-volume half) vs comparison, data: chart_data/talk_tercile_gain.json

**So what:** The shift looks deliberated: argued out on talk pages and then not reverted. That points researchers and journalists to talk archives (RfCs, consensus closes) as the place where the outcome was decided.

**Caveat:** Talk volume is counted for the whole window and may be a result of contested additions as well as a cause; 90 comparison articles.

## 5. Contested words get in by persistence: most stuck only after several failed tries  [SUGGESTIVE]

**What we see:** In the leads of 25 topic key articles, critical-term insertions survive 30 days only 36% of the time. In 15 comparison key articles the figure is 56%. Of the 35 topic article-term pairs where a term finally stuck, it took on average 3.3 failed attempts by 3.4 different editors, and 66% stuck only after retries; in comparison articles the average was 1.4 failed attempts. Over the window, critical terms entered 25 topic article-terms and left 6. Comparison articles were roughly balanced: 4 entered, 5 left.

**Chart:** survival curve (1, 7, 30, 90 days); x: days after edit, y: share still present, series: insertions vs removals x topic vs comparison, data: chart_data/ratchet_summary.json, chart_data/archetypes.json

**So what:** On these pages a single edit rarely settles anything. The outcome favours the side that keeps returning, and over 2021-26 that was mostly the side adding critical terms. Readers should treat a lead as a snapshot of an ongoing contest. Platforms could watch for terms that keep coming back after removal.

**Caveat:** Each lead revision is a binary presence of a term; one rewrite can flip several terms at once.

**Examples:**
- *Israel*: 'apartheid' entered and left the lead 39 times; median survival of each state was about 12 hours; still flipping in Sept 2026

## 6. The fight is cooling: insertions that used to be reverted within days now stick  [SUGGESTIVE]

**What we see:** The share of topic critical-term insertions still in place 30 days later was 19% in 2023 (n=90). It rose to 42% in 2024, 46% in 2025 and 77% in 2026 (n=13). Lead flips peaked at 129 in 2023-Q4 and fell to 7-13 per quarter in 2026. Removals also became stickier (21% in 2023, 50% in 2026), so pages are settling in general, not only in one direction.

**Chart:** line chart; x: year (or quarter), y: 30-day survival of insertions and removals; second panel: flips per quarter, data: chart_data/survival_by_year.json, chart_data/flips_per_quarter.json

**So what:** The contested wording is now freezing in place, mostly in its post-war form. The window in which these leads were decided looks like late 2023 to 2025.

**Caveat:** 2026 has few events, and the most recent events have less time to show 30-day survival (censored events are excluded).

**Examples:**
- *Zionism*: definition 'colonial' in/out 44 times, stable since 2026-08-27 (known)

## 7. A handful of permanently hot articles host most of the fight; most articles change in one quiet step or not at all  [SUGGESTIVE]

**What we see:** Four articles account for 68% (354/517) of all critical-term lead flips in the 25 topic key articles: Israel (178), Gaza war (89), Gaza genocide (50), Hamas (37). The median topic article had 8 flips, close to the median comparison article (7). Of 275 topic article-term pairs: 113 were frozen, 9 changed in a single step, 9 were tried and reverted, 17 went back and forth (3-5 changes) and 26 were tug-of-wars (6 or more). Across the deep panel, hotness persists: the rank correlation of pre-war and post-war edit volume is 0.88. After the war the top 5% of topic articles took 34% of edits; for comparison articles the figure was 23%.

**Chart:** stacked bar of archetypes per group, plus a Pareto curve of flips by article; data: chart_data/archetypes.json

**So what:** The 'Wikipedia war' visible in the news is fought on about 4-5 pages. The quieter, wider shift (cards 1 and 8) happens elsewhere, in single uncontested edits. Studying only the famous battlegrounds would miss most of the change.

**Caveat:** Key articles were chosen because they are prominent, so they are hot by construction.

**Examples:**
- *Gaza genocide*: slow creep: 2024-01 'Allegations have been made that the 2023 Israel–Hamas war constituted a genocide' -> 2025-01 '... state that Israel has committed genocide' (summary: '"state" is more neutral than "accuse"') -> 2025-10 'The Gaza genocide is the ongoing, intentional, and systematic destruction of the Palestinian people' (summary: 'per talk')
- *Israel*: tug-of-war: nakba/expulsion 50 flips, apartheid 39, occupation 32 in the lead

## 8. Quiet pages absorb the change; battleground pages resist it  [STRONG]

**What we see:** Mass tier, articles in the top three activity quintiles, split by revert rate within each quintile. Low-revert topic articles gained critical terms 28.5% of the time (n=780). Mid-revert gained 13.9% and high-revert 13.1%. Comparison articles show the same direction but much weaker: 10.5%, 10.3%, 6.5%. The gap between topic and comparison is largest in the low-revert third (2.7x).

**Chart:** grouped bar; x: revert-rate tercile, y: share gaining critical terms, series: topic vs comparison, data: chart_data/revert_tercile_gain.json

**So what:** This refines 'revert-heavy articles change less'. Most of the topic-specific shift landed where nobody fought back. Monitors focused on edit wars will miss it, and the uncontested pages are the place to audit.

**Caveat:** Revert rate is partly an outcome (contested insertions create reverts), so this is not a causal effect.

## 9. After Oct 2023 the topic was written by a narrower, more established crowd  [SUGGESTIVE]

**What we see:** Edits made by accounts new that quarter fell from ~25% (2022-Q1 to 2023-Q3) to 12-17% (2024-Q4 to 2026-Q3) in topic articles. It spiked at 41% in 2023-Q4. Comparison articles stayed at ~25-34%. Anonymous edit share fell from 17% to 5% in topic articles, versus 20% to 9% in comparison. Topic articles have more talk-page edits per edit (0.67 vs 0.50), rising to ~1.0 in 2024-Q2/Q3. The median top-10 editor share is 0.50 for topic articles and 0.35 for comparison.

**Chart:** line chart; x: quarter, y: newcomer edit share (and anon share), series: topic vs comparison, data: chart_data/who_edits_by_quarter.json

**So what:** This fits Wikipedia's extended-confirmed restriction on the Arab-Israeli conflict area, which keeps new accounts out. The post-war text was therefore written mainly by experienced regulars, not by a flood of newcomers. 'Brigading by new accounts' is not what these numbers show.

**Caveat:** We have no protection logs; the link to extended-confirmed rules is an inference.

## 10. A small cadre writes the definitions, but the timing does not look orchestrated  [SUGGESTIVE]

**What we see:** Eight editors changed first sentences on 3 or more topic key articles, making 22% of the 208 lasting changes. One of them is a bot (Monkbot). Comparison articles have none. In a matched 15-article topic subsample there are still ~4.3 such editors, covering 16% of changes. These regulars made 5 of the 9 first-sentence changes that added critical terms. Their summaries are mostly MOS and copyedit work (29%, vs 14% for other editors). Synchrony tests come out null. No editor pair edits within 30 minutes of each other on 5 or more articles in the deep sample, and the shuffled null also gives 0. In the mass tier, recurring pairs are rarer after the war (topic 1.6 per 10k edits, then 0.7; comparison 0.6, then 0.35). Multi-article 'sweeps' are rarer on topic pages than comparison pages (2.2 vs 4.5 per 1,000 editors). One signal did rise. The article sets of the 150 most active topic editors overlap 1.61x more than a popularity null (1.11x before the war). Comparison editors went from 1.17x to 1.08x.

**Chart:** small-multiples dot plot of signal ratio vs null; x: pre/post, y: observed/null, series: topic vs comparison, data: chart_data/coordination_signals.json, chart_data/edit_summaries.json

**So what:** The picture is an interest-based community: topic-area regulars watching the same pages. It does not look like a scheduled campaign. These signals cannot detect off-wiki coordination, shared views, or sockpuppets, and they cannot rule them out.

**Caveat:** Editor hashes are salted per run, so editors cannot be linked across files; watchlists and breaking news also produce overlap.

**Examples:**
- *Israeli apartheid*: 2024-08-05, regular editor: 'taking the first step in summarizing the body in the lede to reflect the article's new scope' -> 'Israeli apartheid refers to a system of systematic discrimination...'

## 11. Editors give the same kinds of reasons on both sides, and few cite talk-page consensus  [ANECDOTE]

**What we see:** Across 208 lasting topic first-sentence changes, 7% of summaries cite talk or consensus, 9% sources, 12% policy (NPOV/DUE/MOS), 17% copyedit, and 18% are empty. The 88 comparison changes are similar: 8%, 7%, 9%, 10%, 22%. Directional changes are few: 9 add critical terms, 6 remove them, 4 remove hedges, 3 add them. Hedge removals cite policy in 3 of 4 cases. Changes that add critical terms often present themselves as routine ('clarifying', 'improving the first three paragraphs').

**Chart:** stacked bar of reason categories; x: group / direction, y: share of summaries, data: chart_data/edit_summaries.json

**So what:** The edit-summary record shows little visible negotiation. If the deliberation happened, it happened on talk pages (which have 0.67 talk edits per article edit). That is the place to look next.

**Caveat:** Keyword coding of short summaries; directional n is tiny.

**Examples:**
- *Hamas*: 2024-01-11 'extremely unconventional way of saying the territories are occupied' -> 'Israeli-occupied Palestinian territories'
- *Zionism as settler colonialism*: 2023-10-23 'Redundancy for the sake of boldface... See MOS:AVOIDBOLD' -> 'has been described as a form of settler colonialism'

## 12. How big the news spike was does not predict whether the lead changed  [ANECDOTE]

**What we see:** Across 22 topic key articles, the Oct-Nov 2023 pageview spike ranged from 2.7x (Judaism) to 77x (Hamas). Spike size is unrelated to the number of critical terms that entered the lead (Spearman 0.01). It is only weakly related to how many flips happened (0.30). Hamas had the second-largest spike and 35 flips but gained no term. Israel had a 10x spike, 103 flips and gained 2 terms.

**Chart:** scatter; x: pageview spike (log), y: post-war critical-term flips / terms gained, data: chart_data/spike_vs_change.json

**So what:** Attention brings activity, not a direction. Which way a page moves depends on who is editing it and what they bring to it, not on how many readers arrive.

**Caveat:** Only 22 articles.

# New questions

### Q1. Did extended-confirmed protection (ECP) cause the drop in newcomers, and did lead flips fall when protection levels rose?

- **Answerable now?** Partly. The newcomer share fell from ~25% to ~15% in topic articles; comparison stayed flat (card 8). Protection timing is not in our data.
- **Needed:** action=query&list=logevents&letype=protect&letitle=<title> (protection history), or prop=info&inprop=protection for the current state. Pair it with the existing ratchet flips.
- **Feasibility:** 1-2 requests per article; ~10k requests for the mass tier, 40 for key articles.
- **Why:** Separates platform rules from editor behaviour as the driver of who writes the text.

### Q2. Are critical terms added by older, high-edit-count accounts, and removed by newer ones (or the reverse)?

- **Answerable now?** No. Editors are salted hashes, and the hashes cannot be linked across runs.
- **Needed:** Usernames for the revids in ratchet.json events (prop=revisions&revids=...&rvprop=user), then list=users&usprop=registration|editcount|groups. Hash immediately after joining.
- **Feasibility:** ~520 event revids, so ~11 requests at 50 per batch, plus ~5 user batches. Trivial.
- **Why:** Tests whether one side of the tug-of-war is the veteran establishment.

### Q3. Is the post-war edit pattern a transfer of a pre-existing community, or did the topic recruit editors from other areas?

- **Answerable now?** No.
- **Needed:** For the top 200 topic editors: list=usercontribs (namespace 0, 2021-2026), then classify the topics of the articles they edit.
- **Feasibility:** ~200 users x 5-20 requests each, so about 2-4k requests.
- **Why:** Distinguishes 'regulars got busier' from 'new recruits arrived'. Speaks directly to the organic-vs-organised question.

### Q4. Do contested wordings settle through formal talk-page processes (RfC closes) rather than through attrition?

- **Answerable now?** Weak proxy only: 7% of lasting summaries cite talk or consensus. A quick check of Gaza genocide: its final 2025-10 step cites 'per talk'.
- **Needed:** Talk-page revision history (prop=revisions on Talk:<title>, rvprop=timestamp|comment|size), with a search for 'RfC' and 'closed' in comments. Talk archive text via action=parse. Align each close date with the stabilisation date in the ratchet data.
- **Feasibility:** 5-50 requests per key article (archives), so ~1k for 40 articles.
- **Why:** If RfCs end tug-of-wars, the outcome is a governed decision. If not, persistence decides.

### Q5. Do critical insertions have a higher automated revert-risk score than removals, and do the survivors differ?

- **Answerable now?** No.
- **Needed:** Wikimedia LiftWing revertrisk-language-agnostic: POST https://api.wikimedia.org/service/lw/inference/v1/models/revertrisk-language-agnostic:predict with {rev_id, lang:'en'} for each ratchet event revid.
- **Feasibility:** ~520 requests, one per revid.
- **Why:** Shows whether the platform's own quality tools treat the two directions symmetrically.

### Q6. Does a title move (e.g. 'Allegations of genocide...' to 'Gaza genocide') come before the lead dropping its hedging?

- **Answerable now?** Not directly. Bisect shows the lead voice changed in steps over 2024-25, but we have no move dates.
- **Needed:** list=logevents&letype=move&letitle=<title> (and the old titles); prop=redirects.
- **Feasibility:** 1-3 requests per article.
- **Why:** Renaming may be the lever that later forces the first sentence to follow (MOS:FIRST). Several summaries say 'Post RM clean up' or 'per body'.

### Q7. Is the drop in insertion reverts after 2024 (card 5) just exhaustion, or did the sources change? Do sticking insertions coincide with new high-profile sources (UN, ICJ, Amnesty reports)?

- **Answerable now?** Partly. 30-day survival by year: 19% (2023), 42% (2024), 46% (2025), 77% (2026).
- **Needed:** Reference lists at each stick revision (prop=revisions&rvprop=content&revids=...), then diff the refs added in the same edit.
- **Feasibility:** ~1 request per event revid (~520).
- **Why:** Tells apart 'sources moved and the text followed' from 'one side outlasted the other'.

### Q8. Does the change spread from hot hub articles to linked peripheral articles with a lag?

- **Answerable now?** No. The mass tier has only 3 snapshots.
- **Needed:** prop=linkshere for the 5 hub articles; the revision history of the linked articles (rvprop=timestamp|ids, then content at the first critical insertion).
- **Feasibility:** ~5 requests for links, then ~20-50 per periphery article for ~500 articles.
- **Why:** Card 6 suggests the quiet periphery carries most of the change. Diffusion would show how.

### Q9. Does talk-page volume, rather than reader attention, predict which articles end up changing?

- **Answerable now?** Yes, done (now card 4). Topic talk-per-edit is 0.67 vs 0.50 for comparison. Ran it: topic articles with 20 or more edits (n=296), split into thirds by talk edits per article edit. Share gaining critical terms: {'low': 0.222, 'mid': 0.408, 'high': 0.596}.
- **Needed:** Nothing new: talk_edits and dcrit are in results.json.
- **Feasibility:** 0 requests.
- **Why:** Tells apart 'deliberated change' from 'quiet change'.
