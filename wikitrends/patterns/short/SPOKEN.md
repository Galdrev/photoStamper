# Spoken version (about 90 seconds)

We asked a simple question. Did English Wikipedia's articles on Israel, Palestine, Zionism and Judaism change their story? (pause)

On average, they barely moved. (pause)

But picture a big shop. Walk the aisles, and almost nothing has changed. Then look at the window display by the front door. (pause) The window display is different now.

The opening paragraph of an article is that window. It is what readers, search engines and AI assistants see first. Only about four in a hundred of these articles gained what we call critical vocabulary there: words from the legal and historical charges made against Israel, like genocide, apartheid or ethnic cleansing. (pause) But those few pages get fifteen percent of all the reading.

Here is one window. In 2021, the Nakba "was the destruction of Palestinian society and homeland in 1948". Today it "is the ethnic cleansing of Palestinian Arabs by Zionists". (pause) Neither version is neutral. Both state a baseline.

What surprised us: take out every sentence about recent events, and nearly ninety percent of the change is still there. The war sent editors back to 1948. (long pause)

It was not one-way. Antisemitism now appears in fifty-four opening paragraphs, up from thirty-seven. Some critical terms were taken out.

Long-standing editors made the changes, not a wave of new accounts. Articles on Judaism did not move. And as of late 2026, the wave has passed. (pause)

We can't say who is right. We can say the front door changed.

# Elevator version (about 20 seconds)

On average, Wikipedia's articles on Israel, Palestine and Judaism barely changed. (pause) But a few hundred of the most-read Israel and Palestine pages now open differently, mostly retelling 1948. Long-standing editors made the changes, in one wave that, as of late 2026, has passed. We can't say who is right. We can say the front door changed.
