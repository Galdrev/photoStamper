# The average didn't move. The shop window did.

**The short answer:** Partly: most articles, and all of Judaism, stayed put, but a few hundred heavily read Israel and Palestine pages now open with a different baseline story, centred on 1948, the land and words like genocide and ethnic cleansing.

## The image to remember: the shop window

Walk the aisles of a big store: almost nothing has changed. But the front window, which every passer-by sees, has been redressed. Only 3.9% of topic articles gained critical words in their opening paragraph, yet those pages get 15% of topic reading. The opening paragraph is the window: it is what search engines and AI assistants quote.

Zionism, 2021: "an ideology and nationalist movement among the Jewish people that espouses the re-establishment of ... a Jewish state". Zionism, 2026: "an ethnic nationalist movement ... to establish and support a Jewish homeland through colonization in the region of Palestine".

## Five things that matter

1. **A few windows changed, not the whole store.** Out of 4,642 topic articles, the change sits in a few hundred, including some of the most read.
   *Visual: two bars, "share of articles" next to "share of reading".*
2. **The story moved back to 1948 and the land, not to the war.** Remove every sentence about recent events and 89% of the extra change remains.
   *Visual: the Nakba's opening sentence, 2021 above 2026.*
3. **It came as one wave, and the wave has passed.** New critical words poured in through 2024; by 2026 the pace was back to pre-war levels.
   *Visual: one hump on a timeline, 7 October 2023 marked.*
4. **Quiet pages changed most, not the famous edit wars.** Rarely reverted pages gained new wording far more often than fought-over ones.
   *Visual: three bars, rarely to constantly reverted.*
5. **This was not just "what wars do to Wikipedia".** At the same level of editing, articles on other conflicts changed less often.
   *Visual: bars by subject, Palestine and Israel above Ukraine and Armenia.*

## Why you can trust this

We wrote the test down before seeing the data, and when the first answer came back "not supported" we said so. Every result is compared with 5,678 similar articles on other countries and conflicts, so normal churn cancels out. The main result held on half the data, kept sealed until the end. We filtered out sentences about recent events. And we dropped our own results when they failed, including a measure of how 1948 departures are described.

## What this does not mean

- **Not proof the new wording is true or false.** Counting words measures emphasis.
- **Not proof of bias or intent.** We see what changed, not why.
- **Not proof of coordination.** We found no synchronized editing, but cannot rule out off-wiki planning.

## Questions people ask

**So is Wikipedia biased?** The data show which story is now the baseline, not whether it is right. Changes also ran the other way: antisemitism now appears in 54 topic opening paragraphs, up from 37. Lopsided, not one-sided.

**Who did this?** Established editors: 9.1% of wartime edits came from accounts with under 500 edits, against 17.2% on comparison pages.

**Is it just because of the war?** Battles brought editors; court rulings did not. But what they rewrote was mostly older history.

**Did it only happen in English?** English and Portuguese opening paragraphs moved. Most others showed no clear change.

**Does it matter? Does anyone read it?** Yes, the changed pages are the read ones. But 83% of topic reading still lands on opening paragraphs that did not change.
