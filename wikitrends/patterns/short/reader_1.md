# Reader 1 (secondary-school teacher, no prior views). Read order: C, A, B

## Draft C

1. Main finding: After October 2023 a few hundred English Wikipedia pages on Israel and Palestine, some very widely read, started using words like genocide and ethnic cleansing more, mostly when retelling 1948. Judaism pages did not change and the burst is over.
2. Quiz
   - Q1: Yes, but only "a few hundred" articles. The articles that changed get 15% of reading. I can't tell what share of all articles that is (the draft didn't make this clear).
   - Q2: Israel and Palestine pages changed; Judaism pages did not.
   - Q3: Mostly not war news. 89% of the extra change is in sentences about older events.
   - Q4: After Oct 2023, one wave, over by 2026.
   - Q5: No. It says outright it can't show bias, intent or coordination.
3. Confusion: "Articles whose opening paragraph gained critical words get 15% of topic reading, well above the comparison share." I had no idea what the comparison share was, so 15% meant nothing. Also "A test fixed in advance, averaged over all articles, found no clear rise." That sentence contradicts the "partly" headline and I couldn't tell how. "Sealed half" needed the glossary.
4. Tomorrow: Few pages changed, but the read ones, and it was about 1948, not the war.
5. Ratings: clarity 7, fairness 7, memorability 5, trust 7.

## Draft A

1. Main finding: On average almost nothing changed. But a few heavily read Israel and Palestine pages now open with different, more accusatory wording, mainly about 1948. This happened in one wave in 2024, by experienced editors, and Judaism pages did not move.
2. Quiz
   - Q1: Yes. 85% of articles unchanged; 3.9% changed, but they carry 15% of reading. Clear (the visual descriptions carry the numbers).
   - Q2: Israel and Palestine pages up, Judaism flat.
   - Q3: Mostly not war news (89%, older history and the land).
   - Q4: Wave peaked in 2024, now over, and the new wording remains. Very clear.
   - Q5: No. Not intent or coordination, and wording is not true or false.
3. Confusion: "A curve showing how concentrated the change is (a Lorenz curve), four lines..." and "+2.22 per 10,000 words... (+1.98)". I lost interest at the Visual blocks, which are full of numbers (7.0M, 5.3 to 7.7, 13.5, 16.0...). Probably instructions for a designer, but as a reader it was a wall. "statistically the same as zero" was fine, but "Lorenz curve" lost me.
4. Tomorrow: "The front door changed." The Nakba then and now.
5. Ratings: clarity 6, fairness 7, memorability 7, trust 8.

Note: the "30-second version" at the end is the best recap in any draft. "Some changes ran the other way, and most new labels arrived attributed ('according to')" is a useful fairness line. "Why you can trust this" was the most credible, including "We dropped our own weak results".

## Draft B

1. Main finding: Most of Wikipedia's coverage of this topic stayed the same, but a few hundred heavily read Israel and Palestine pages had their opening paragraph rewritten. It now centres on 1948 and uses words like genocide and ethnic cleansing. It is like a shop window being redressed while the store is unchanged.
2. Quiz
   - Q1: Yes, but only 3.9% of articles, which are 15% of reading. And 83% of reading still hits unchanged paragraphs. Very clear.
   - Q2: Israel and Palestine changed, Judaism "stayed put". Zionism example makes it concrete. Quiet pages changed more than famous edit-war pages.
   - Q3: Not mainly the war. Removing recent-event sentences still leaves 89%.
   - Q4: One wave through 2024, back to pre-war pace by 2026.
   - Q5: No. Not proof of bias, intent or coordination.
3. Confusion: "At the same level of editing, articles on other conflicts changed less often" (point 5). I wasn't sure what "same level of editing" meant. The spoken version duplicates the top, so it felt long. "Redressed" quietly suggests someone did it on purpose, which sits oddly with "not proof of intent".
4. Tomorrow: The store is the same, the window is not.
5. Ratings: clarity 8, fairness 7 (slightly higher than others thanks to the antisemitism line, slightly lower for the intentional-sounding metaphor, net 7), memorability 9, trust 7.

## Comparison

I'd send B to a friend. The shop window gave me a picture that held all the numbers, and it answered "so does it matter?" straight away. It also did the best job of balancing the scary headline with "83% of reading is on unchanged paragraphs" and "Lopsided, not one-sided".

Steal from the others:
- From A: the closing "30-second version" and the "front door" line. Also the Judaism-flat evidence and "Some changes ran the other way, and most new labels arrived attributed". Also "We dropped our own weak results" up front, and the banned-accounts fact (13.7% vs 12.3%), which pre-empts the obvious "was it troublemakers?" question.
- From C: the glossary (B uses "critical words" without defining it) and the "what the study did not show" list, kept short. C's "sealed half" is jargon, so use A's or B's wording.
- Fix in B: explain "the average didn't move" in one plain sentence, since the headline says "partly" yet B never says what the null result was. Define "comparison articles" once, and give the Nakba 2021 vs 2026 pair alongside the Zionism one. Reconsider "redressed" so it does not imply intent.

Best 3 lines to steal:
1. "The store is the same. The window is not." (B)
2. "Lopsided, not one-sided." (B, antisemitism 37 to 54)
3. "We can't say who is right. We can say the front door changed." (A)
