How English Wikipedia retold Israel’s story, 2021–2026

Zionism, “through colonization”. Israel “began committing genocide”. How the reference the world reads now tells Israel’s story.

English Wikipedia is the text millions of people read; the Israel article alone had 4.6 million views in the last 12 months. That article now says, in Wikipedia’s own voice: “After the Hamas-led October 7 attacks, Israel began committing genocide against Palestinians in Gaza.” The next sentence is Israel’s denial. Zionism is now a movement “to establish and support a Jewish homeland through colonization in the region of Palestine”. The change sits in the articles people read first: since July 2023 one in ten Top-importance articles on Israel, Palestine and Jewish topics took on this vocabulary, against 4.4% on other conflicts, while the long tail moved no more than elsewhere. The test we wrote down first, an average over all articles, found no rise (How far to trust this). We start with one sentence.

CONTENTOne sentence, 292 edits

Zionism was the “re-establishment” of a Jewish state. Now it is “a Jewish homeland through colonization”.

Bottom line: The encyclopedia’s one-line answer to “what is Zionism?” no longer calls it a return; it now says a homeland “through colonization”, and nearly every attempt to take that word out was undone (37 of the 41 removals whose before-and-after text is stored).

In January 2021 English Wikipedia defined Zionism as a movement for “the re-establishment of and support for a Jewish state in the territory defined as the historic Land of Israel”. Today it is “an ethnic nationalist movement that emerged in late 19th-century Europe to establish and support a Jewish homeland through colonization in the region of Palestine”. Where both versions of the sentence are stored, “colonial” or “colonization” was written into the first sentence 44 times and taken out 41 times, all since June 2024; 37 of those 41 removals were undone, and the word has stood since 27 August 2026.

CONTENTThe charge 150 most-read articles that existed in 2021 · exact sentences

Israel “began committing genocide”: a charge absent in 2021 is now stated as fact. The share of blame barely moved.

Bottom line: Israel’s share of the blame barely moved (23.6% to 26.4%) even as every side drew more harm sentences; what changed is the charge. Genocide, absent in 2021 and in July 2023, is now stated as fact in the opening sections of Israel and Benjamin Netanyahu.

We coded every sentence about violence, harm, displacement, legal charges or rights in the 150 most-read opening sections that existed in January 2021, at three dates: January 2021, July 2023 and now (5 November 2026). The share that names Israel, Israelis, Jews or Zionists as the one doing the harm was 23.6%, 24.1% and 26.4%: a change of +2.8 points (range −1.9 to +8.3), inside the margin of error. The charge changed. Genocide: 0 sentences in 2021, 0 in July 2023, 7 now, plus Israel’s denial. Two are in Wikipedia’s own voice. In the Israel article the next sentence is “Israel has denied that its actions constitute genocide.” The Netanyahu article says “Netanyahu's government has been orchestrating the Gaza genocide, culminating in the South Africa v. Israel case before the International Court of Justice (ICJ) in December 2023” and carries no denial.

More apartheid, killing, labels, occupation, attribution

Newer articles such as October 7 attacks, Gaza war and Gaza genocide are not in this count; the next section covers them. These are simply the most-read pages, so Hitler, the Holocaust and Anne Frank sit next to Israel, Hamas and Gaza.

The word “genocide” has stood in the Israel article since 4 May 2025; eight earlier insertions from November 2023 did not last, the longest 18.9 days. Until October 2026 the denial read “Israel and its allies, including the United States, deny...”; the United States has since been dropped. Five sentences attribute the charge to scholars, international lawyers or rights groups; two of those use verbs that present it as settled (Palestine: “recognised as constituting genocide ... by a growing scholarly consensus”; Gaza–Israel conflict: “Many scholarly sources and international organizations have concluded”). Apartheid: 0, 1, 4 sentences, one in Wikipedia’s own voice. Apartheid is an older layer: across the topic articles the word rose 39% from 2021 to 2023 and 7% after. Killing and massacre charges rose on both sides: against Israel 9, 10 and 26 sentences; against Hamas or Palestinians 8, 8 and 23, nearly all in Wikipedia’s own voice. The encyclopedia did not stop saying that Hamas kills Israelis. What faded on that side is the label: one overview article’s “terrorist attacks by Palestinian militants” became “Palestinian political violence”. Own-voice “terrorist” is not reserved for one side: Baruch Goldstein is now “a Jewish terrorist and mass murderer”. Among these 150, no sentence charges Hamas or Palestinians with genocide or apartheid at any date. Outside them, the October 7 attacks article, created in 2023, carries an attributed one: “Some have alleged that the attack was an act of genocide or a genocidal massacre against Israelis.”

Occupation is more often stated in Wikipedia’s own voice: 16 of 35 occupation charges against Israel in 2021, 18 of 35 in July 2023 and 44 of 55 now (46%, 51%, 80%). The same move happened to Russia in Crimea and Turkey in northern Cyprus, on small counts (2 of 8, 7 of 10, 14 of 19: 25%, 70%, 74%). Many of the new ones are history (the 1967 war, Suez) and events of 2024 to 2026; the legal-status sentences that changed are in the West Bank and Israel opening sections (“under Israeli occupation, which is illegal under international law”; “Israel's illegal occupation of the Palestinian territories”). Since the July 2024 ICJ advisory opinion, editors have a court to cite. Occupation charged to Russia in Crimea and eastern Ukraine, and to Turkey in northern Cyprus, went 25%, 70%, 74%. The 50 most-read articles on other disputes (Ukraine, Karabakh, Northern Ireland, Kosovo, Cyprus, Kashmir) gained no new genocide charge against any state; their 5 genocide sentences are historical. This is context, not a matched control: none has a war on the Gaza scale with an ICJ genocide case.

What this means: genocide went from absent to stated as fact in the Israel and Netanyahu opening sections, which no other dispute we compared saw. Stating genocide in Wikipedia’s own voice is common on atrocity articles (of 11 we checked by hand, an anecdote, not a sample, 4 did so in 2023 and 9 do now); Gaza differs in speed and reach, not in the label itself. “Own voice” is a coding judgement on exact sentences; it says what the encyclopedia states, not who is right.

New critical terms were attributed more often than the old ones: across the 10,000-article crawl the share with a source named rose from 21% to 25% in topic articles, while comparison articles stayed at 17%. Still, three in four loaded words are stated in Wikipedia’s own voice. A few flagship opening sentences later dropped the attribution, as Gaza genocide did (next section).

CONTENTOctober 7, Hamas and the hostages

October 7 is “armed incursions” in the first sentence. The hostages have never been in it.

Bottom line: The facts of October 7, the toll, the hostages and the massacres, are in the opening section, but its first sentence calls the attack “a series of coordinated armed incursions”, and the hostages have never reached that sentence.

The encyclopedia’s one-line answer to “what was October 7?” is “a series of coordinated armed incursions from the blockaded Gaza Strip into the Gaza envelope of southern Israel were carried out by Hamas and several other Palestinian militant groups” (since 8 October 2026). Across 107 versions, “massacre” stood in that first sentence for about an hour in total, “terror” for 0.2 days, the death toll for 1.2 days, and the hostages never.

Further down, the opening section says Hamas was “massacring civilians in 21 communities” (sentence 4), that “at least 1,195 people were killed” (sentence 7) and that “251 Israeli and non-Israeli civilians and soldiers were taken as hostages to the Gaza Strip” (sentence 9). It once said “Israel and international media have reported rape and sexual assault by Hamas fighters” (1 January 2024); it now says “Numerous sources have reported dozens of cases of rape and sexual assault”, naming no perpetrator (2 November 2026). The article’s own short description, shown in search results and apps, says “2023 armed incursions and massacres in Israel”, and sub-articles such as Be’eri massacre say “massacre” in their first sentence: the main sentence is a choice, not a lack of sources.

Hamas. “Militant” left the definition on 12 October 2023, replaced by “military”, five days after the attack, in the month the article was read 8,236,474 times. Hamas is now “a Sunni Islamist Palestinian nationalist political organisation with a military wing known as the al-Qassam Brigades”. “Terrorist” was written into the definition five times; the longest lasted about 2.4 hours. The sentence naming the countries that designate Hamas a terrorist organisation moved from sentence 5 of 20 (2021) to sentence 22 of 23 (October 2026), and by 31 October 2026 named no country (the edit falls between 1 and 31 October and may be reverted): “Several countries have designated Hamas as a terrorist organisation.” Neither the Hamas nor the Israel opening section mentions the hostages.

More sexual violence, Hannibal, the Gaza war, Gaza genocide

The killing is described plainly in the second paragraph of the October 7 article (“massacring civilians in 21 communities”), and the opening section gives the toll (at least 1,195 killed), the hostages (251) and that the governments of 44 countries described the attack as terrorism. On 1 January 2024 the opening section said “Israel and international media have reported rape and sexual assault by Hamas fighters with documentary footage and photographs, which Hamas has denied.” On 2 November 2026 it says “Numerous sources have reported dozens of cases of rape and sexual assault.” That sentence no longer names who committed them. It now also says the IDF used the Hannibal Directive that day, which “may have led to at least 13 Israeli civilian deaths”. In October 2026 a clause calling Israel’s response genocide was removed from sentence 2. Since 26 May 2025 the Gaza war’s first sentence has named Hamas for less than two days in total; Hamas, the toll and the hostages are in sentence 2. The same day, the October 7 article’s title dropped “Hamas-led”. We found nothing linking the two edits, and the shorter title matches common usage.

Hamas: “militant” left on 12 October 2023 and came back three times (1 to 2 August 2024; 19 to 31 August 2024, as “militant wing”; 12 to 18 February 2025); it has been absent from the whole opening section since 11 December 2025. The five “terrorist” insertions add up to under six hours in three years. “Resistance movement” got in once, on 1 August 2024, and was gone the next day. The “Several countries” edit is days old and may be reverted.

Gaza genocide. The first sentence went from “Allegations have been made that the 2023 Israel–Hamas war constituted a genocide against the Palestinians” (1 January 2024), through “accused” and “according to”, to Wikipedia’s own voice. The current sentence has stood since 26 September 2025: “The Gaza genocide is the ongoing, intentional, and systematic destruction of the Palestinian people in the Gaza Strip carried out by Israel during the Gaza war.” Since then, 10 edits tried to bring back attribution; each was gone within 45 minutes.

Gaza genocide: short-lived own-voice wordings came and went from July 2024. Before the own-voice step, the first sentence had named each outside finding as it appeared: Amnesty International (December 2024), Human Rights Watch (December 2024), B’Tselem (July 2025), the International Association of Genocide Scholars (August 2025) and the UN Commission of Inquiry (16 September 2025). The opening section still gives Israel’s view near its end: “Israel and its supporters maintain that its actions do not constitute genocide”. The reverted attribution attempts read, for example, “has been described by most scholars as” and “the alleged ongoing”. The genocide charge against Hamas for October 7 stays attributed: “Some have alleged that the attack was an act of genocide or a genocidal massacre against Israelis.”

What this means: the facts about October 7 are in the opening sections, often in fuller form than in 2024. What moved is their place and their grammar: the first sentence says “armed incursions” and leaves out the hostages; Hamas is a political organisation first; the designation is near the end; and the genocide charge against Israel is stated as fact in the Gaza genocide, Israel and Netanyahu articles, while the one against Hamas is “alleged”.

Nine armed groups, chosen by hand, not a sample: “terrorist” also left the first sentences of Islamic State, al-Shabaab and Boko Haram, so that word follows Wikipedia-wide style on contentious labels. Hamas, Hezbollah and Palestinian Islamic Jihad are now introduced as political or paramilitary bodies, while Islamic State and al-Qaeda are still introduced as militant organisations, and their designations sit in the last fifth of the opening section. Hamas and Hezbollah do also run political parties.

Across topic opening sections, terror vocabulary fell by about a fifth after July 2023 (−24% and −19% in our two article samples) while comparison pages stayed flat; Hamas alone lost 47 mentions. Suggestive: the fall sits in a few articles.

Notes phases, undone changes

“Terrorist” also left the first sentences of Islamic State, al-Shabaab and Boko Haram; what sets Hamas, Hezbollah and Palestinian Islamic Jihad apart is “militant”, and their designations sit in the last fifth of the opening section.

Bottom line: In Wikipedia’s telling of 1948, Palestinians were now driven out by “Zionists” or “Zionist forces” (the Nakba, the 1948 Palestine war, the 1948 Arab–Israeli War), while “Hundreds of thousands of Jews fled or were expelled from Arab states” with no one named; one 1948 article, on the expulsion and flight, removed its agent.

In the Nakba article, “ethnic cleansing” entered Wikipedia’s own voice on 5 March 2024; the perpetrator was named on 10 August 2025, worded “by Israel” from 30 December 2025 and “by Zionists” from 6 July 2026. PROCESSTiming

One wave: editing rose with the battles, not the court rulings, and the pace of new wording has fallen back

Bottom line: The new vocabulary arrived in one wave that followed the fighting, not the courts: it peaked in 2024 at about 16 new critical terms per 1,000 topic edits and is now back at or below the pre-war rate of about 7 (chart below); the new first sentences of Zionism and Israel have stood since 27 August and 4 September 2026.

New critical terms per 1,000 topic edits ran at about 7 before the war, 13.5 in late 2023, and 16.0 and 15.9 through 2024. Then the rate fell to 10.8 and 8.2 in 2025, and to 5.1 and 5.9 in 2026, at or below the pre-war rate. Israel’s first sentence has stood since 4 September 2026, Zionism’s since 27 August. PROCESSOrganised or organic? Exploratory · not pre-registered

Canvassing is documented on both sides. Our tests found no campaign footprint, and could only have seen a large one.

Bottom line: Wikipedia’s arbitration committee documented off-wiki canvassing on both sides of this subject; our data show regulars returning to the same pages, not a campaign footprint, and the footprint test could only have seen a very large effect.

The edits were made by long-standing accounts, in small groups per page, with insertions and removals done by different hands, and a pool of regulars moving across the hot pages: 9 accounts made 48 of the 216 traced first-sentence changes on the 25 key articles by changing 3 or more of them; on the comparison key articles no account did. Our data cannot see off-wiki channels; it can see timing and footprint. The wave of critical wording began in the 16 weeks after 7 October 2023, when topic insertions ran at 3.4 times the comparison rate (0.5 times before the war, 2.0 times while the Discord channel was active), before the documented pro-Palestinian channel opened in January 2024 (ArbCom’s April 2025 finding of other coordination gives no start date); the pro-Israel email canvassing ArbCom found dates from the same weeks but aimed the other way; and no campaign, exposure or sanction date leaves a mark we can tell from chance.

That last test has low power: it could only have seen the ratio fall to 0.20 times or rise to 4.2 times. “Not detected” means the test could not see it, not that it did not happen. Only accounts with 500 edits and 30 days may edit these articles.

  • DocumentedDecember 2024, pro-Palestinian. Editors sanctioned for editing after canvassing requests on the Tech for Palestine Discord channel and for “encouraging other users to game the extended confirmed restriction”; one site-banned, one topic-banned.

2 · Timing and footprint: what our own tests can see

The footprint test, in one line

For each exposure and sanction date we compared topic and comparison insertions in the four weeks before and after, against 2,000 random dates. No single date passes a 5% test; averaged over the five dates the ratio is 1.01 (p = 0.53): no common footprint.

3 · Accounts that move together, measured against chance

We tested every pair of the 697 accounts whose critical-term edits we can see in topic opening paragraphs (847 on comparison pages) against chance. On topic pages 14 pairs moved together beyond chance, each on at least three of the same articles, where chance gives about 2 (5 at most in 95% of shuffled worlds); on comparison pages 3 pairs, against 0.28, and no group.

More the two blocs, and what they are not

What this means: a few small sets of experienced editors kept returning to the same contested pages, more often than chance explains. Subject regulars with shared watchlists look like this, and so would a coordinated group; this test cannot tell them apart. It found none of the tighter signatures of a campaign: edits in step, pasted wording, or a burst inside a documented canvassing window.

  • Bloc A: four accounts, adding and removing. 23 articles; 45 insertions and 23 removals of critical terms. None of 200 shuffled worlds made a group this large and tight. Medium confidence.
  • Bloc B: four accounts, found when we test insertions alone. 114 insertions across 53 articles. 1 of 200 shuffled worlds made a group like it. Medium confidence. A test on insertions alone cannot see what the same accounts removed.
  • What the blocs are not. No member edited within 10 minutes of another; on the same article, members came a median of 14 days (A) and 66 days (B) apart. No sentence was pasted into five or more articles, on topic or comparison pages; the Nakba wording on 17 Palestinian village pages came from 15 accounts, piecemeal. The test on removals alone found no bloc. All eight accounts are long-standing (median account age about 3 years in A and 6 in B), and none has under 500 edits, which the Arab–Israeli restriction requires; ArbCom’s December 2024 case concerned gaming that threshold.
  • Timing, low confidence. Bloc A made 41% of its edits in the war’s first months (October 2023 to January 2024, also the period of the pro-Israel email canvassing ArbCom found), against 18% for matched sets of random accounts (p = 0.02 to 0.04), and 21% in the pro-Palestinian Discord window (January to September 2024), against 23%. Bloc B: 34% against 15% (p = 0.05), and 29% against 24%. No result passes our strict level (p < 0.01), and the email window is also the first months of the war, when all accounts edited more. Being active in a window does not make a group part of what ArbCom found.

The data cannot see talk-page and RfC votes, where canvassing is usually alleged, accounts that were never caught, or text outside the opening section. Three results point the predicted way (the January 2024 motions, PIA5, the April 2025 bans) and none survives correction for 13 tests.

The accounts were veterans new to these pages, and the same pages gained protection and formal discussions (appendix: Signals of coordination). The tests cannot see off-wiki channels, talk-page votes, or accounts that were never caught.

How we chose the articles three samples, two limits

How we chose the articles

Wikipedia’s volunteer editors sort articles into subject groups called WikiProjects. Each group grades how central an article is to its subject: Top, High, Mid or Low importance, or leaves it ungraded.

We took the four groups that cover this story: Israel, Palestine, Judaism and Jewish history. We measured them twice.

  • The core crawl. The four groups grade about 9,200 to 9,500 articles Top, High or Mid (the count depends on how redirects and shared pages are handled). We analysed 4,642 of them: every Top and High article and a random draw of the Mid ones, against 5,670 mostly Top and High articles from 23 groups on other countries, conflicts and religions. Most of the edit-by-edit findings on this page come from here.
  • The probability sample of the full populations. All 48,974 articles the four groups assess, at every grade, and all 141,356 articles assessed by nine groups on other conflict regions (Ukraine, Turkey, Serbia, Kosovo, Armenia, Azerbaijan, the Kurds, Cyprus and Northern Ireland). We drew 3,000 articles at random from each side, within each group and grade, and weighted each one back to the true size of its group. This is where the core-versus-long-tail result comes from.
  • A random sample of all English Wikipedia: 2,466 articles drawn by Wikipedia’s own random-page function, to check for site-wide drift. There was none.

Two limits.

  • Against other active conflicts, such as Ukraine or Armenia–Azerbaijan, the gap is smaller. The share of reading that lands on changed opening paragraphs is about the same as there.
  • The 25 to 40 famous articles we trace edit by edit, such as Zionism, Israel and Gaza genocide, were chosen by us because they are contested. They show how change happened. They are not a sample of Wikipedia.

Weighting the core crawl to its population was exploratory, computed after the fact. The probability sample was designed for population estimates, but its estimands were not pre-registered, so we report them as strong evidence rather than a confirmatory test.

“Not detected” is not “did not happen”. Several tests on this page found nothing: no footprint of any campaign, no tight pairs of editors, no average rise. Each could only see large effects, and none can see what happens off Wikipedia, in talk-page votes, or through accounts that were never caught. Where a test had low power we say so next to the result.

Methods in one paragraph

We counted fixed lists of critical and Jewish-history terms in the opening paragraphs of 10,312 sampled articles (4,642 topic and 5,670 comparison), across their revision histories. Each result is the change in topic articles minus the change in comparison articles. That subtraction removes ordinary editing trends. We computed two ranges for each result: one by resampling articles, one by resampling WikiProjects. Where the two disagree, we call the result suggestive. Half the articles were held back until the end. The main result replicated there: +3.6 percentage points, with a range of 1.7 to 5.6, for those Top, High and Mid articles. A later probability sample of all 48,974 topic articles and 141,356 articles on other conflict regions, weighted to the true size of each group and grade, shows the gap at Top importance (+5.9 points) and none in the long tail (+0.4 points overall, within the margin of error). A random sample of 2,466 Wikipedia articles showed no site-wide drift. Measures that failed precision checks, including one on 1948 departures, were withdrawn.

Limitations and what this cannot tell us
  • Accuracy. Word counts measure emphasis, not correctness.
  • Intent and coordination. We see what changed, not why. We cannot see coordination that happened off Wikipedia.
  • The average. A test fixed in advance, run on a smaller panel, found no clear average rise. Across the full population of 49,000 articles the average change is no larger than for other conflict regions. The change is concentrated in the core.
  • Readers. We know what text was live, not what readers took from it.
  • Languages. English and Portuguese moved. Nine other languages showed no clear change.
What would change these conclusions
  • A renewed rise in the quarterly insertion rate.
  • A similar rise in a random sample of all Wikipedia articles. We ran that check: 2,466 random articles showed no drift (−0.06 critical terms per 10,000 words, 0.0% gaining).
  • Known false matches: 76 of 410 net added Nakba/expulsion hits are in Judaism and Jewish-history articles, about Jewish expulsions (Edict of Expulsion +20).
  • A much lower reading share once the few largest pages are removed.
Glossary
  • Opening paragraph: the summary at the top of an article.
  • Comparison articles: other countries, conflicts and religions, measured the same way.
  • Jewish-history vocabulary: homeland, indigenous, antisemitism, Land of Israel. We counted these too.
  • Own voice vs attributed: "it is genocide" versus "X states it is genocide".

Show the method data, groups, text cleaning, the test, limits
Data
Every revision since 1 January 2021, from the Wikipedia API. A full-text snapshot of each article on the first day of every quarter. Talk-page activity and monthly pageviews. Collection ran on GitHub Actions.
Groups
Topic articles are hand-picked plus a random sample from Zionism, antisemitism, conflict and Jewish-history categories. Comparison articles cover other nationalisms, states, conflicts, genocides and religions. Comparison articles closely tied to the conflict are reported but excluded from the tests.
Text
References, templates, files and back matter are stripped before counting. So citation titles do not add to the counts. The opening paragraph (the lead) is everything before the first section heading. Snapshots under 300 words are not measured. For the opening paragraph the cut-off is 40 words.
Test
Difference-in-differences on the balanced panel of articles that existed in January 2021. We take each article’s change in rate and average it by group. The estimate is topic minus comparison. A permutation test with 5,000 shuffles gives p. p is corrected with Benjamini–Hochberg within the primary and the secondary family. 2,000 bootstrap resamples give the 95% interval.
Limits
Counting words measures emphasis, not accuracy. “Genocide” can grow because a court case exists, not because of bias. Word lists miss paraphrase and sentence-level stance. Category-sampled articles vary in quality. Editors are reported only in aggregate. No claim is made about any editor’s intent.

PROCESSThe ratchet Exploratory · not pre-registered

Do added words stay? The battleground pages only

We followed every saved version of the opening paragraph of key articles. When someone adds a critical term, how long does it last? When someone removes one, how long does the removal last? If additions stick and removals are undone, the wording moves one way, like a ratchet. This section covers the key battleground articles only. The sampled crawl has 4,642 topic articles. Across those, 47.3% of added terms were still in place after 30 days. In comparison articles the figure was 42.9%. So the ratchet does not describe the topic as a whole.

PROCESSOrganic or organised? Exploratory · not pre-registered

Signals of coordination: the rest of the record

The bloc tests are in the canvassing section; the co-editing bars, the 30-minute test and who the accounts are follow below. Here are the deep-panel signals. Organic change looks like many people making small edits over time. Organised change looks like a few people editing many articles quickly, often close together. We compare each pattern with what you’d expect by chance. Chance here means the same editors and articles, with the timing shuffled.

Read this first

  • The same patterns appear when many editors watch the same pages, or when big news breaks.
  • These are signals, not proof of coordination.
  • Editors are anonymised. No individual is identified or accused.

Who the accounts are, in aggregate

  • Many hands, no single author. In the Zionism first sentence, 14 different accounts made the 16 traced changes that carried each quarter’s new wording. No account made more than two.
  • Veterans, new to the page. War-time editors of the key articles had a median account age of about 9 years. Only 7% to 13% of them had edited the same page before the war. The war drew experienced Wikipedians new to these pages.
  • Regulars, not tag teams. 9 accounts changed the first sentence of 3 or more key articles, making 48 of 216 traced changes; on the comparison key articles no account did. No pair of accounts repeatedly edited within 30 minutes of each other across five or more articles, before or after the war, in either group.

What this means: adding and removing contested words is done by largely separate sets of accounts, on topic and comparison pages alike: of 673 article-accounts on topic pages, 318 only inserted, 296 only removed and 59 did both. A single revert pair produces that split, so it says nothing about viewpoint. On pages with 10 or more critical-term flips, the most active account made a median 32% of them on topic pages, against 20% on comparison pages (16 and 24 pages). A pool of regulars and shared watchlists produce the same pattern as organised editing would; our data cannot tell them apart, and cannot see who any account belongs to.

The same pages gained formal process and structure

Extended-confirmed protection went from 10.9% of topic pages in 2021 to 31.1% now (comparison: 0.7% to 5.0%). Formal discussions rose from 0.29 to 0.79 per 100 articles a year, on 51 pages. Framing categories were added to 7.2% of topic articles, against 2.2%. Pages already linking to a core concept such as the Nakba picked up its vocabulary more often at the same editing volume.
