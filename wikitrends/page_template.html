Did Wikipedia’s articles on Jews, Israel and Zionism drift toward an anti-colonial framing?

We read every change made to hundreds of English Wikipedia articles over almost six years. We compared articles about Israel, Jews and Zionism with articles about other countries, conflicts and religions.

What this study can tell you

  • Whether words like “colonial”, “apartheid” and “genocide” became more common in these articles.
  • Whether that change was bigger than in articles about other countries and conflicts.
  • When the wording changed, and in which sentences.

What it can’t tell you

  • It measures how wording changed. It does not check whether the wording is true.
  • It can’t tell who is right about the conflict.
  • Patterns in who edits, and when, are not proof that people worked together.

The robustness checks are in the scoreboard above. A check holds when its estimate keeps the main result’s sign and is clearly different from zero: p < 0.05 (R1, R2, R7), or a range that excludes zero (R4). The median check (R5) has no p and no range, so it only asks whether the sign stays the same. R3 holds when the post-war yearly extra change is bigger than every pre-war year. The check (R6) is the reverse: it holds when ordinary geography and climate words show no difference.

PROCESSThe ratchet Exploratory · not pre-registered

Once a loaded word goes in, does it stay?

We followed every saved version of the opening paragraph of key articles. When someone adds a loaded word, how long does it last? When someone removes one, how long does the removal last? If additions stick and removals are undone, the wording moves one way, like a ratchet.

PROCESSOrganic or organised? Exploratory · not pre-registered

Was the editing many-handed and gradual, or concentrated and fast?

Organic change looks like many people making small edits over time. Organised change looks like a few people editing many articles quickly, often close together. We compare each pattern with what you’d expect by chance: the same editors and articles, with the timing shuffled.

Read this first

  • The same patterns appear when many editors watch the same pages, or when big news breaks.
  • These are signals, not proof of coordination.
  • Editors are anonymised. No individual is identified or accused.

Same rules, different people

German, French, Hebrew, Arabic and other Wikipedias follow the same rules and cover the same events, but different communities write them. If every language moved the same way, events probably drove the change. If English moved on its own, the community may matter more. For researchers

How this was measured

The full method, the word lists and the limits. Open the sections you need.

Show the method data, groups, text cleaning, the test, limits
Data
Every revision since 1 January 2021 from the Wikipedia API, a full-text snapshot of each article on the first day of every quarter, talk-page activity and monthly pageviews. Collection ran on GitHub Actions.
Groups
Topic articles are hand-picked plus a random sample from Zionism, antisemitism, conflict and Jewish-history categories. Comparison articles cover other nationalisms, states, conflicts, genocides and religions. Comparison articles closely tied to the conflict are reported but excluded from the tests.
Text
References, templates, files and back matter are stripped before counting, so citation titles do not add to the counts. The lead (opening paragraph) is everything before the first section heading. Snapshots under 300 words (40 for the lead) are not measured.
Test
Difference-in-differences on the balanced panel of articles that existed in January 2021: each article’s change in rate, averaged by group, topic minus comparison. A permutation test gives p (5,000 shuffles), with Benjamini–Hochberg correction within the primary and the secondary family. 2,000 bootstrap resamples give the 95% interval.
Limits
Counting words measures emphasis, not accuracy. “Genocide” can grow because a court case exists, not because of bias. Word lists miss paraphrase and sentence-level stance. Category-sampled articles vary in quality. Editors are reported only in aggregate, and no claim is made about any editor’s intent.
